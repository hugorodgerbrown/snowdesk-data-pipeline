Four ways to draw the fall line

A skier cutting across a forty-degree face and a skier pointing straight down it are having two different days. On a map they look identical — same line, same colour, same steepness underfoot. This is four attempts at telling them apart: three that failed, and the one that works.

the problem

Colour says how steep the ground is, not what you are doing on it

A saved route is painted by the steepness of the ground beneath it. That is a real and useful thing to know, and it is silent on the question that decides the day. Two tracks over identical ground get identical paint: the one that traverses the face, and the one that commits to it.

The difference is not a detail. A traverse spends a few seconds above the same slope a descent spends a minute on, with a fraction of the speed and a fraction of the load on the snow.

what we already know

The answer is already in the data, and nobody had drawn it

For every 25 m of a route we know two things about the ground: how steep it is, and which way it faces — which is to say, which way is downhill. We also know which way the track is heading. The angle between “which way is downhill” and “which way am I going” is the entire question.

It has been computed for years, in one narrow place: the stretches over fifty degrees, where a slip does not stop. Below that — the thirty-five to forty-five band where most decisions are actually made — the question was never asked.

Four ways to say it

All four drawn from the same tour, on whichever leg you picked. A leg is a stretch that is consistently climbing or consistently descending — the run between two turning points.

Three words

tried, rejected

Down the fall line, up it, or across it — pick one per stretch.

▼ down the fall line▲ up it— across it nothing at all below thirty degrees

What it says

One word per stretch, from the angle between where the track is heading and where the ground falls. It borrows the vocabulary already used on the steepest ground, and asks the same question lower down.

It is the cheapest of the four and the easiest to explain, which is why it was built first.

What it costs

  • Two hidden numbersNothing is drawn below thirty degrees, and a second rule decides where “across” begins. Neither is visible to the reader.
  • A cliff edgeA crossing and a descent two degrees apart look nothing alike. The ground does not change at that boundary; only the drawing does.
  • It saturatesOn a descent, nearly every stretch clears the gate, so the row fills edge to edge and stops discriminating. Pick leg 2 or 4 above and watch it happen.

How closely, as a quantity

tried, rejected

Not a word. A height.

above · descending the fall line below · climbing it at the midline · a pure traverse

What it says

How closely the track follows the fall line, drawn as a size rather than rounded to a word. Full height is straight down it, the midline is a pure traverse, and every value in between gets its own height. No boundary, no cliff edge, nothing hidden.

What it costs

  • It is abstractNothing on the strip is a number anyone has used. “Six tenths of the way to the fall line” is not a thing said at the top of a pitch.
  • It collapsesOn a descent almost everything is near the fall line, so the strip is a solid block. Removing the boundary did not remove the problem the boundary was causing — it moved it.

Worth seeing for yourself. Pick leg 2. The strip goes solid. That is not a drawing fault: it is what this measurement looks like on a descent, and it is why “continuous” is not automatically better than “three words”.

Two steepnesses

built, then put down

How steep is the ground — and how steep is it going to feel?

the ground’s angle the steepness along your track the gap between them is the traverse

What it says

Both numbers, in degrees, on one scale. The upper line is the ground. The lower line is the slope you will actually stand on, which is gentler than the ground whenever you are not pointing straight down it.

Where the two lines touch, you are on the fall line. Where they separate, you are cutting across. The size of the gap is how much you are cutting across by — so the thing that was a word, then a height, is now simply the distance between two lines you wanted to read anyway.

On this tour the ground under the steep stretches averages forty-three degrees; the track over that same ground averages sixteen. That gap is the whole tour’s character in one figure, and no other drawing here shows it.

What it costs

  • NoiseThe track’s own steepness is worked out from recorded heights every 25 m, and those wobble. Raw, this tour throws a spike at eighty-five degrees — a slope nobody skied, and nobody could.
  • SmoothingSo it is averaged over a short window before being drawn. That is a chosen number again, which is exactly the thing the other options were criticised for.

The difference is which number is chosen. A boundary that decides whether a stretch is “across” or “down” changes the answer. A smoothing window changes how jagged a line looks. Both are choices; only one of them can tell you the wrong thing.

Words, only when you ask

tried, rejected

Draw nothing. Say it in a sentence when the reader points at something.

leg 2, halfway along — 34° · across the fall line

What it says

Nothing, until asked. The route keeps the colour it already has, and the relationship gets named in the panel beside it — where there is room for a sentence instead of a glyph, and where nothing has to be compressed into eight pixels of height.

It is the honest minimum, and for a while it looked like the right answer.

What it costs

  • No scanning“Where on this descent am I committed to the fall line?” takes a swipe along the whole leg, one point at a time.
  • Wrong jobThat question — seeing the shape of a thing at a glance — is the one the strip exists to answer. A strip that answers it only on request is not doing the job.

Three things we drew on the map and threw away

Before any of this reached a strip, it was tried on the map itself — an arrow beside the route, pointing the way the ground falls. It failed three times, and each failure was more interesting than the attempt.

An arrow offset to one side. Legible, and meaningless. Placed beside the line it reads as a direction-of-travel marker — which is precisely the thing it is not. Every reader we imagined would take it for “go this way”.

An arrow anchored on the track, pointing true. Exact, and invisible. Where the track runs straight down the fall line, the arrow lies along the track, underneath its own outline, and disappears into the line it was annotating. The clearer case is the one it cannot draw.

A row of glyphs under the track. On a descent nearly every stretch qualifies, so the row fills up completely and becomes a texture. A reader of a textured line learns nothing and stops looking.

What the three have in common

Each failure came from the same place: on a fall-line descent there is no “beside” the track. Below you is ahead of you. The side of the line an arrow sits on is only meaningful when you are traversing — which is the half of the problem it was supposed to explain.

the lessonA mark that needs a side needs the case where there are two sides. Steepness does not: it is a number everywhere, including the case that broke all three arrows.

What happened next

It was built. The colour bar on the rail grew into exactly this chart — the class as a fill under a ground-angle curve, the track’s own steepness as a second line through it — and then it was taken out again.

The reasoning had held and the reading did not. Two more lines in a strip eight pixels tall cost more attention than the answer was worth, and a reader looking for a place on the route now had a graph to parse first. The strip went back to flat colour.

Where the answer came from instead

Not from drawing the question better, but from asking a different one. Borrow the frame a pilot uses: pitch is how far the nose is down, roll is how far you are banked over. A traverse is roll. A fall-line descent is pitch. And the two decompose the steepness of the ground exactly, which turns the whole problem into one quantity that nothing had ever drawn.

Better still, the profile already draws pitch — so only one new mark is needed, not two. That is taken apart in a companion page, Pitch and Roll on a Ski Track.
