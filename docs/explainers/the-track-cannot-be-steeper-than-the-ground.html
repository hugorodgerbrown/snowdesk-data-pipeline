Snowdesk · how a route is measured

The track cannot be steeper than the ground

A saved route knows two slopes at every point: how steep the ground is, from a terrain model, and how steeply the track climbs or falls along its own length, from the heights the recording device wrote down. The second can never be larger than the first. When it is, the recording is wrong — and that one fact is the whole of the check.

Two slopes, one bound

A skin track zigzags up a steep face precisely so that it is not as steep as the face.

tan(track) = tan(ground) × cos(heading off the fall line)

The ground's angle comes from a terrain model on a 5 m grid, measured at the middle of every 25 m piece of the route. The track's gradient is the change in the recorded height across that piece, over its length.

A cosine is never more than one, so the track's gradient is never more than the ground's. Straight down the fall line they are equal. Every other heading is gentler. That is not a rule anyone chose; it is what walking on a surface means.

Why the two differ at all

  • GroundHow steep the slope is, whichever way you face on it. It decides whether it can avalanche.
  • TrackHow steep the line you took was. It decides how hard the day felt, and it is what a height profile draws.
2

Where the track's heights come from

From the stored route — and from nothing finer, because nothing finer is kept.

When a GPX file is uploaded, it is read, turned into a list of points with a height on each, and thrown away. The file itself is not stored. That keeps less of anyone's location history in fewer places, and it means there is no file store to run.

It also sets a ceiling. The track's gradient is worked out by walking the stored points again, 25 m at a time, and reading the height at each step. Whatever is wrong in those points stays wrong, and nothing can go back to the original recording to check.

What the stored points are

  • Up to 2,000 pointsThe recording exactly as the device wrote it. All four tours here are this size.
  • Over 2,000Thinned to fit, keeping the points that best preserve the line's shape. See section 5 for what that does to a gradient.
3

A break in the recording

On one tour, three consecutive points fall 360 m in height while moving 5 m across the map.

Nobody descends 360 m in five metres. Somewhere around those points the device lost its height — a barometer re-calibrating or a satellite fix dropping out, for example — and when it came back it wrote down a number from a different altitude. The recording has no way to say so. It is simply the next point.

stored heightthe points either side of the break

The obvious fix is to smooth: average each piece's gradient with its neighbours so a single bad point is diluted. Averaged over five pieces — about 125 m — a 360 m drop is still a 71° slope. Smoothing does not remove the break. It spreads it.

The rule

If a piece of track is more than 5° steeper than the ground under it, it is not a measurement of the track.

Such a piece is rejected: it gets no gradient, it says why, and it is left out of its neighbours' averages so it cannot leak into them. Everything else is then smoothed as before.

The 5° is an allowance for honest error, not a tuning knob. A 2 m error in one height — ordinary for a watch or a phone — tilts a 25 m piece by about 4.6°. The ground's angle is itself an average over 10 m of terrain, so a short steep step reads a little gentler than it is. Below 5°, the rule would be throwing away noise as if it were a break; well above, it would let breaks through.

What rejection does not do

  • RepairThe break is still in the stored route. The rule declines to report a gradient there; it does not invent one.
  • Unknown groundWhere the terrain model has no answer, there is no angle to check against, so nothing there is rejected — a break over unsurveyed ground passes through.

What it can never be better than

A gradient read from stored points inherits everything that happened to them on the way in.

Thinned tracks run steep on switchbacks. A long recording is thinned to 2,000 points by keeping the ones that best hold the line's shape. On a zigzag, the corners are what goes. The heights at the kept points are real, but the distance between them is now the straight cut across the corner rather than the zigzag that was walked. The same climb over a shorter run is a steeper gradient — overstated in exactly the shape a skin track is made of. The four tours measured here are all under the limit, so their figures do not include this.

Breaks are permanent. Because the original file is not kept, a break in the recording is in the route for good. A better rule written later applies to new uploads only.

A trusted pitch should come from the ground. The angle you descend along your skis can be worked out from the terrain alone — the ground's angle and which way you face on it — without reading a single recorded height. Where a figure has to be relied on, that is the one to use.
