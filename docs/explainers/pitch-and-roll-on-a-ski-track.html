Pitch and roll on a ski track

Borrow the frame a pilot uses. Pitch is how far the nose is down — the angle you descend along your skis. Roll is how far you are banked over — the angle across them, which is what a traverse physically is. It turns out not to be an analogy: the two take a slope apart exactly, and only one of them has ever been drawn.

the identity
tan²pitch + tan²roll = tan²slope

Pitch and roll are the two short sides; the steepness of the ground is the long one. Point straight down the fall line and every degree of the slope shows up as pitch and none as roll. Traverse it dead level and every degree is roll. Every real track is somewhere between, and where it sits is the thing no drawing had ever said.

This is not a rule of thumb. Checked against the tour above, place by place: 508 measurements, largest reconstruction error 0.000000000000014 degrees. It is arithmetic, not approximation.

why it is worth having

It halves the drawing. Pitch is the steepness of the line on the elevation profile — already on the screen, already read at a glance. So the frame asks for one new mark, not two, and that mark carries something the profile has never been able to say.

It is a number people already have a body for. Nobody has an instinct for the angle between a compass bearing and the direction a slope faces. Everybody who has stood on a hill knows how far over they are leaning and how much edge they are holding. That is roll, and it is the same number.

1

Reading a tick

If the mark is a drawing of the skis, there is no key to learn.

The proposal is a short tick across the track at every place, leaning away from upright by the bank angle. Upright means no bank at all — you are pointing straight down the fall line. Laid flat means a dead-level traverse. Everything else is in between and you read it the way you read a clock hand.

Each row below is one place on a slope. The first panel is the ground from above, contours and all, with the track crossing them. The second is the same place seen from in front, looking along your skis — that tilt is the bank you would actually feel underfoot. The third is the tick.

What the sample shows

Reading down it: the same 40° face produces no bank at all when you point straight down it, 23° when you angle 30° off, 36° when you cut across at 60°, and the full 40° when you traverse it dead level. Take the heading and steepen the ground to 50° and the bank goes to 46°.

Gentle ground limits itself. A 20° face can only ever produce 20° of bank, however you cross it, so the row stays quiet on ground that does not matter without anything having to be switched off. Every other attempt at this needed a threshold to do that job, and a threshold is a number the reader cannot see.

One wrinkle, stated plainly

The tick is the front-on view turned upright. Drawn literally — flat for a fall-line descent — a row of them along a horizontal strip would collapse into a dashed line, and on a steep tour most places are near the fall line.

The angle survives the turn. Lean away from upright is still exactly the bank; it is measured from the vertical instead of the horizontal. A tick 30° off upright is 30° of edge underfoot.

2

Along a real leg

Where on this descent am I committed, and where am I cutting across?

A leg is a stretch of a route that is consistently climbing or consistently descending — the run between two turning points. Pick one and the ribbon draws every place on it.

Leg

What you are looking for

Not the individual ticks — the clusters. A run of upright ticks is a stretch committed to the fall line. A knot of leaning ones is a traverse, and on a climb they come in a regular rhythm because that rhythm is the switchbacks.

The descent on this tour reads as mostly committed, with three distinct places where the line cuts across instead. That is a fact about the day that no colour, no arrow and no wording had previously put on the screen.

Small rules

  • No tickGround the terrain model has no answer for. An absent mark is not a claim of "flat".
  • ColourOnly past about 25° of bank, so the eye is drawn to the stretches where it matters rather than to the whole row.
  • No extra heightThe ticks define the track line by crossing it, the way sleepers define a railway, so the row costs almost nothing.
3

The shape of a whole leg

Never mind where — what kind of leg is this?

Every place on the leg as one point: bank across, pitch up. The arcs are constant slope angle, so how far a point sits from the corner is how steep the ground is, and which way it lies from the corner is what the track is doing with it. A committed descent piles up the left-hand edge. A rising traverse spreads along the bottom. You cannot find a place on it, but you can see the character of a day in one glance.

Move across the cloud and the instrument on the left flies to that point.

What it costs

A sign. Banked left or banked right is a claim about which shoulder the ground falls away on, and it is exactly as easy to get backwards as it is important to get right. A drawing that leans the wrong way does not look broken — it looks confident, and points at the wrong side of the track.

Every earlier attempt at this question failed in the same family of ways, and all of them came down to the same fact: on a descent straight down the fall line there is no "beside" the track, because below you is ahead of you. Bank sidesteps that — a lean needs no side to be on — but the sign still has to be settled and tested rather than assumed.

What it does not need

No threshold, no new vocabulary, nothing stored. The bank angle falls out of two numbers already known for every 25 m of a saved route: how steep the ground is, and which way it faces. It is worked out when the page is drawn and thrown away afterwards, which means the drawing can be re-tuned without anything being recalculated across every route ever uploaded.

Describes a design decision taken in September 2026. The bank ribbon is the proposal, not yet the app; three earlier attempts at the same question were drawn and put down first.

Every drawing on this page is rendered live from one real 12.9 km tour — its track, its heights, and the legs cut from them are the genuine article. The slope angles are illustrative, chosen to give a realistic spread of steepness rather than measured off that particular mountain.
