Anatomy of a route

A saved route started as a purple line on the map. It now carries six kinds of information about the ground it crosses, packed into a line a few pixels wide and a small popup. This page takes it apart, one layer at a time.

Every picture below is a real screenshot of the running app, using the swisstopo winter basemap. The route is a demonstration loop drawn through real steep ground above Verbier, and its terrain was sampled from the same 5 m grid the app uses for everyone. For each picture, every map layer was hidden except the ones named, so it shows what a single layer adds.

RULE ONE

Steepness comes from the ground, never from the track

A skin track zigzagging up a 38° face rises about 15° along its own length, and a traverse across it rises almost nothing. Colour either by the track's own gradient and a serious slope looks easy. So the angle is always read from the terrain under the line, and the heights recorded in the file are never used for it.

RULE TWO

Height comes from the track, never from the ground

Where the skier is is exactly what the uploaded file records. When a route is compared with a bulletin's elevation band, the height is the track's own recorded height. The two rules look contradictory, but they share one principle: take each figure from the thing that actually measures it.

0

The track, as recorded

August 2026

Where did I go, how far, and how much up and down?

An uploaded GPX is stored as a list of points — longitude, latitude and height — thinned to at most 2,000 of them. Distance, ascent and descent are measured once, at upload, on the full-resolution track. The map only receives the thinned geometry, so it never recalculates those totals; if it did, the popup would show a second, slightly smaller set of numbers for the same route. How a file becomes that list is a page of its own: From GPX to Line.

The height profile is drawn from the third number of each point the map already holds, so it needs no extra request and works offline.

What to read

  • Purple lineA route of yours. A route shared with you but not yet saved is dashed and a different colour.
  • Dot / flagStart and finish. They appear from zoom 10.
  • Profile gapThe file had no height at that point. The line breaks there instead of being joined across the gap.
  • No ascent shownThe file had no heights at all. This is shown as "we don't know", never as 0 m.

The terrain the line is measured against

20 August 2026

How steep is the mountain here, whatever line I take?

This isn't strictly part of the route. It's the map overlay the route's colours are matched to. It uses the bands the SLF recommends: 30–35°, 35–40°, 40–45°, 45–50° and over 50°. Ground under 30° is left clear. Most slab avalanches release between 30° and 45°, so the first three bands matter most.

Everything a route says about terrain in layers 2–5 comes from the same kind of source: a terrain model measured against the mountain, never the heights in the uploaded file.

2

The steepness of the ground under the line

14 September 2026

Along my line, where is the steep ground, and how much of it is there?

When a route is saved, a background job walks the track in 25 m steps. For each step it asks the terrain model how steep the ground is at the middle of that step, averaged over a 10 m window. The answers are stored with the route, one per step, together with the direction each slope faces (its aspect). The map draws nothing with the aspect yet, but layers 3–6 all depend on it.

The colours are the overlay's colours, value for value, plus one more. The terrain overlay can leave gentle ground clear, but a line can't go transparent without looking broken, so ground under 30° is drawn sky blue.

The popup's height profile uses the same colours, and a second line of figures summarises the ground. Those figures are calculated once, when the route is sampled, so the popup, the routes list and the trip page always agree.

Three states that must stay distinct

  • Flat purpleNever sampled. Nothing has checked the terrain, so nothing is claimed.
  • Grey dashesSampled, but the terrain model had no answer (outside coverage, for example). This never means gentle ground.
  • A colourAn angle was measured. A reading of exactly 35° counts in the 35–40° band, so boundary readings never land in the gentler band.

A flat traverse can be drawn steep. A path cut across a face is level underfoot, but the terrain model describes the hillside around it. So a level stretch of a tour can be coloured 40° or 50°, which is the steepness above and below you.

3

Key passages: the ground above you

14 September 2026

Where could the slope above me release onto me?

The line's colour answers "how steep is the ground I'm standing on?". A ring answers a different question: could the terrain around me release a slab I could trigger? A rising traverse along the foot of a 40° face is gentle underfoot, and it's exactly the kind of passage a ring is for.

For each sample point, the sampler looks uphill (the opposite of the stored aspect) at 40 m and 80 m, along three directions 45° apart. A step is flagged if its own ground or the steepest ground found uphill is 35° or more. The uphill check is what adds the cases the line's colour misses. Each unbroken run of flagged steps gets one ring, at its midpoint. Where the ground is too flat to have a direction, it checks all four compass directions at 80 m.

Rings depend only on the terrain, never on the bulletin. A regional forecast can be a danger level out for one particular slope, so a ring based on the ground alone stays useful even when the forecast is wrong. Skitourenguru grey out their equivalent markers for the same reason.

What to read

  • RingLook harder here. It isn't a severity rating: the map receives only the location, not the angle, so two rings can't be compared.
  • Popup"17 key passages".
  • Key missingIf an outage stopped the uphill checks, the route stores no ring list at all rather than an empty one, so it gets retried. "Nothing flagged" and "couldn't check" stay separate.

Not a complete list. A route with no rings is not a safe route. This check only covers slope angle. Slope size, shape, what lies below and forest cover are all missing, so compare the rings with the slope shading.

4

No-fall passages: the ground underfoot

15 September 2026

Where would a slip not stop, and what is the track doing there?

A no-fall passage is a run of steps on ground of 50° or more. Once a passage has started, it continues over steps of 45° or more, and a passage must be at least 25 m long. Rings and splits usually appear together: ground over 50° has already been flagged by the 35° ring check. They answer different questions, though. The ring says the slope above you can release; the split says you are standing on it.

Each passage gets a word describing what the track does there. The direction of travel along each step is compared with the direction the slope faces. Within 30° of downhill is descending, within 30° of uphill is climbing, and anything else is crossing. This route has both "down the fall line" and "across the fall line".

The word is only a description and never hides a passage. A rising traverse of 50° ground is marked like any other, because you can't afford to fall there either.

How it's built

  • CalculatedWhen the route is requested, from the angles and aspects already stored. Nothing new is stored.
  • WhyStoring the result would make every threshold change require re-sampling every route against the terrain model. Calculating it on request means thresholds can change for free.
  • Never partialIt needs no extra terrain lookups, so an empty list always means "nothing qualified", never "couldn't check".
  • LengthsMeasured from the 25 m step size, not from straight lines between step boundaries. On a switchback the straight line is shorter and would drop real single-step passages.

An unmarked route can still have no-fall ground. Each sample averages 10 m of hillside every 25 m, so a narrow steep step between two gentler samples is smoothed away. The reverse also happens: a marked passage may be a few metres of rock in an easy slope.

5

The fall line: which way the ground falls

15 September 2026

Am I cutting across this face, or pointed straight down it?

Layers 2–4 describe how steep the ground is. This layer shows a direction: the way water would run and a slab would slide. A 40° traverse and a 40° descent are the same colour, but they are very different days out. An arrow shows which one you're looking at.

Each arrow's direction is the stored aspect of that step, sent to the map as a step number and a compass bearing. The server decides where arrows go and how many there are. The map just draws them and never second-guesses which way a face runs.

Placement

  • Only ≥ 30°On near-level ground, a 5 m grid can pick up a false direction from a stream bank or road cutting. An arrow there would be confident about nothing.
  • SpacingOne arrow at the start of each steep run, then one every 250 m, restarting after each gentle gap. One arrow per step would mean 600 on a long tour: unreadable, and a large offline payload.
  • CalculatedWhen the route is requested, like the no-fall passages, and for the same reason.

No arrow is not a claim of flat ground. Where there's no arrow, go by the line's colour. An arrow is also one direction averaged over about 10 m of hillside. On a rolling face or in a gully, the real fall line swings within a ski length.

6

Against the day's bulletin

14 September 2026 · trip page and route panel

On the day I'm going, where does my line cross the terrain the bulletin warns about?

This is the only layer that changes during the day, and the only one that needs a date before it can say anything. It appears on two surfaces, and each gets its date from a different place: a trip has a day of its own, and the map's route panel uses whichever day the scrubber is showing. Tap a saved route on a March morning while looking at February, and you are reading February's bulletin against your line.

For each region the track passes through, the app finds that day's bulletin. For each avalanche problem, it adds up the stretches of track whose aspect (from the terrain) is one the problem lists and whose height (from the track) is inside the problem's band.

A track over a ridge can be in two regions, and on a border it can be covered by two different providers. Each region's stretch is reported under its own bulletin, and neither is chosen over the other.

Deliberately not

  • A scoreNo number comes out. A score quietly leaves out everything it didn't weigh, and the reader can't tell what that was. The app highlights and never suppresses.
  • Kept as an answerNothing is worked out in advance or held on the server: a bulletin changes twice a day, and a stored result would outlive the bulletin it came from. Your device does keep a reading you have already opened, so it is there away from signal — filed under that route and that day, shown behind a line saying when it was saved, and reported as expired rather than redrawn once it is too old to trust.
  • TreelineA band described as "above the treeline" has no altitude, and the app has no treeline model. The bulletin's own wording is shown instead of an invented figure.

How to read the full picture

This is what the map shows by default. Read it in this order: colour first, then marks, then the popup.

  1. 1Colour: how steep the ground under you is. Grey dashes mean no data.
  2. 2Ring: the slope around you can release.
  3. 3Split line: you are on no-fall ground.
  4. 4Arrow: the way that ground falls, compared with your direction of travel.
  5. 5Popup: the same information as numbers and words, with the height profile in the same colours.
  6. 6Trip or route panel: where the line meets that day's avalanche problems.

Every mark says "look here", never "you're fine". The legend and the slope topic in the app's help explain what each absence means, because each layer is easy to read as more complete than it is.

Where the work happens

Measured once, or worked out every time

Only the slow work — asking the terrain model about 25 m of mountain at a time — is done once and kept. Everything derived from those answers is worked out again on request, so a threshold can change without re-measuring a single route. What your device already holds is what lets a saved route still draw when there is no signal.
