The roundel grades the track

Tap a point on a saved route and its card shows a roundel. The outer ring is the ground: which way the slope faces and how steep it is. The inner ring and the marks in the middle are the track: which way you were heading and how steeply you were climbing or descending along your own line. The track has a scale of its own, and this page explains it.

1

The track is not the ground

A skin track crosses steep ground so that the line itself does not have to be steep.

The ground's steepness is what decides whether a slope can avalanche, and the avalanche services grade it in classes: under 30°, then 30, 35 and 40°. Almost every skin track crosses ground in those classes, yet climbs at a fraction of that angle, because it runs across the slope rather than up it.

On the Backside tour, between 8.7 and 9.2 km, the track crosses ground of 30.8° to 60°, steep to extremely steep in the avalanche services' terms. Over the same stretch the track never rises or falls by more than 4.1°. Graded by the ground's classes, that line would read as the steepest part of the day. It was one of the easiest.

So the point card grades the track on its own scale, by the gradient along the line you took over each 25 m segment, and keeps the ground's classes for the ground.

Climbs and descents live at different angles

A skin track is set for skins to grip, so it rarely climbs past 20°. A descent goes wherever the snow allows.

On the Col de la Chaux tour, 180 of the 25 m segments climb and 444 descend; the other 14 are dead level. The steepest climb is 22.2°; the steepest descent is 31.4°. On the descent's bounds, 86 of the 180 climbs would share one step, 5–15°, and nothing would tell a gentle zigzag from a stiff one.

One scale for both directions would put most climbs into one or two steps and leave the rest unused. So each direction has its own bounds. A climb steps up at 5, 10, 15 and 20°. A descent steps up at 5, 15, 25 and 35°. Below 5° either way the track is level.

Five colours, four marks

The centre of the roundel is small. Three chevrons is as many as can be read at a glance.

The inner ring takes one colour per step, five in all. The centre takes a bar for level, then one, two or three chevrons, pointing up for a climb and down for a descent. The two steepest steps share three chevrons, and only the colour tells them apart. The card prints the step's band of degrees next to the roundel, such as "Ascent · 10–15°", so the reading never depends on the colour alone.

Considered and declined · 20 October 2026

One scale of four steps for both directions, 5, 15 and 25°, one colour and one mark per step. It would have matched the chevrons to the colours, but put climbs on the descent's bounds. On the Col de la Chaux tour, three chevrons mark 35 climbing segments today. On the four-step scale they would mark none, because no climb there reaches 25° (the steepest is 22.2°). The five-step scale stands.

In short

The roundel's inner ring and centre grade the track, not the ground: a skin track crosses steep ground at a gentle angle, and the two are kept apart.

Climbs and descents have their own bounds, because skin tracks stay under about 20° and descents do not.

What it shows

Five steps as five colours on the inner ring, and as a bar or up to three chevrons in the centre. The card prints the step's band of degrees beside it, so no reading rests on colour alone.
