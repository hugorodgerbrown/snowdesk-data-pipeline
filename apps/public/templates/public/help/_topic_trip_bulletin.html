The panel itself carries one sentence of caveat, which is as much as a card can hold without burying the figures it is explaining; everything a reader needs in order to judge how far to trust it is here, in the place the rest of the caveats already are. {% endcomment %} {% load i18n %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} A trip page shows where your line crosses the ground each of that day's avalanche problems describes. The bulletin states, for every problem, which aspects and which heights it applies to; your route has been sampled for the direction the ground faces along it, and it carries its own heights from the file you uploaded. Put the two together and you get a sentence like “1.4 km on N and NE, between 2200 and 2800 m — persistent weak layers”. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} It is not a score, and there will not be one. A single number would have to weigh the things it could measure against the things it could not, and hide which was which — and the parts it left out would be invisible to you. What you get instead is the overlap itself, so you can see what was matched and go and read the bulletin that said it. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Three limits are worth knowing. A regional bulletin describes a whole area and can be a danger level out on any single slope, which is why nothing here tells you a slope is fine. Where a problem is pinned to the treeline rather than to a height in metres, the overlap is matched on aspect alone — there is no treeline in our data. And a route that has not been surveyed for terrain shows no panel at all, which means nothing was compared, not that nothing was found. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} A route that crosses two regions is listed under both, longest stretch first. On a national border those can be two different forecasting services reading the same day differently; both are shown, and neither is treated as the answer. {% endblocktrans %}
