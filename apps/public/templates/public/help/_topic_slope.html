{% blocktrans trimmed %} The Slope angle overlay shades the ground by steepness, so you can see how steep a line is without leaving the map. The bands follow the classification the SLF recommends, the same one you will have seen on Swiss maps: 30–35°, 35–40°, 40–45°, 45–50° and over 50°. Ground under 30° is left unshaded. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The first three bands matter most. The large majority of slab avalanches release on slopes between 30 and 45 degrees, which is why that range is the one to find when a bulletin talks about steep terrain in a particular aspect and elevation. {% endblocktrans %}

{% trans "What it cannot tell you" %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The shading is calculated from an elevation model with a 10 metre grid, so it describes the average steepness of each 10 metre square rather than the ground itself. A short steep roll inside an otherwise gentle face will not appear at any zoom, and neither will a small bench in the middle of a steep one. Zoom in far enough and you will see the individual squares — that is the resolution of the data, not a fault in the map. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Coverage does not extend across the whole map. Where the layer has no data its menu row is switched off with a note, but be careful near the edge: unshaded ground outside the covered area has simply not been surveyed, and looks exactly like ground that is under 30 degrees. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The data is stitched together from several countries' national elevation models, and swisstopo, who publish it, note that accuracy is not guaranteed in the places where two of those models meet. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Treat the layer as one input among several, never as a verdict. It says nothing about the snowpack, about what is above you, or about the day's danger rating — and a slope being shaded, or not being shaded, is not permission to ski it. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The data is the “Slope classes over 30°” dataset published by swisstopo, the Swiss Federal Office of Topography. Full attribution is in the colophon. {% endblocktrans %}
