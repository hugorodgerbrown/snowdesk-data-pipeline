This panel is where the layer's shortcomings live. The map legend carries the five class swatches and a link here, and nothing else: the caveats are too long to sit in a legend panel and too important to drop, and a reader deciding whether to trust a shading needs the whole answer rather than the half that fits. {% endcomment %} {% load i18n %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The Slope angle overlay shades the ground by steepness, so you can see how steep a line is without leaving the map. The bands follow the classification the SLF recommends, the same one you will have seen on Swiss maps: 30–35°, 35–40°, 40–45°, 45–50° and over 50°. Ground under 30° is left unshaded. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The first three bands matter most. The large majority of slab avalanches release on slopes between 30 and 45 degrees, which is why that range is the one to find when a bulletin talks about steep terrain in a particular aspect and elevation. {% endblocktrans %}

{# SNOW-1019: a route's steepness is read on the rails, not on the #} {# map line, which is drawn as its legs (SNOW-1017). The trip page's #} {# own map still colours its line by the bands. #}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} A saved route’s steepness is read on the rail below the map. Open a leg and the second rail draws a strip of slope bands in the same classes, one band for each stretch of the same class, under the leg’s height profile; on a trip’s page the route line itself is coloured by them. Either way the colour is the steepness of the GROUND, never of the track: a skin track zigzagging up a steep face climbs gently along its own length, and colouring it by its own gradient would make a serious slope look easy. Where a band is grey and dashed, the terrain data does not cover it — that is the same coverage limit described below, and it never means the ground is gentle. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The same rule runs the other way, and it surprises people: a flat traverse can be drawn steep. A path cut across a face — a piste traverse, a summer track, a bench — is level underfoot while the ground it crosses is not, and the elevation model describes the hillside rather than the few metres you walk on. So a level stretch of a tour can carry 40 or 50 degree colours. That is the slope above and below you, and on a traverse it is the number that matters. {% endblocktrans %}

{# SNOW-1019: the bank ribbon, which replaced the fall-line arrows, #} {# and the no-fall bars. The map line carries no terrain marks now — #} {# no crux rings, arrows or split line — so these rail marks are #} {# where the reader meets them. #}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Under the bands the second rail draws the track as a row of short ticks, the bank ribbon. Each tick leans by how far the ground tilts across the track, towards the side it falls away: upright where the track runs straight up or down the slope, leaning hard where it cuts across a steep face. Switchbacks show as ticks leaning one way and then the other. Where the tilt is not known there is no tick, and no tick is never a claim that the ground is level. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Below the ribbon, a bar marks each no-fall passage: a stretch where the ground you are standing on is over 50 degrees, where a slip does not stop. Press a bar to select it and read its length. The route’s detail names how many there are and what the track does with the slope: down the fall line, up it, or across it. A rising traverse of 50 degree ground is marked like any other, because it is no place to fall either. {% endblocktrans %}

{% trans "What it cannot tell you" %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The shading is calculated from an elevation model with a 10 metre grid, so it describes the average steepness of each 10 metre square rather than the ground itself. A short steep roll inside an otherwise gentle face will not appear at any zoom, and neither will a small bench in the middle of a steep one. Zoom in far enough and you will see the individual squares — that is the resolution of the data, not a fault in the map. {% endblocktrans %}

{# SNOW-964: and the caveat the passage bars cannot carry. About #} {# the ROUTE SAMPLING — a 10 m analysis window walked every 25 m — #} {# and deliberately not a restatement of the raster paragraph above, #} {# which is a different measurement of a different thing. #}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} An unmarked route is not a route without no-fall ground. A route’s steepness is sampled every 25 metres, and each sample is the average slope over about ten metres of hillside, so a narrow steep passage between two gentler samples is smoothed away before anything measures it. The same goes the other way: a stretch marked as no-fall may be a few metres of rock in the middle of an easy slope. The marks say where to look, and nothing more than that. {% endblocktrans %}

{# And the caveat the ribbon cannot carry: the tilt is read from one #} {# direction per ten metres of hillside, which is a different #} {# averaging from the ANGLE's in the paragraph above. #}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} A tick’s lean comes from one downhill direction for about ten metres of hillside, so it describes the shape of the slope rather than the ground at your feet. On a rolling face, a gully, or a ridge the real fall line swings either side of it within a ski length, and on convex ground it changes as you cross. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Coverage does not extend across the whole map. Where the layer has no data its menu row is switched off with a note, but be careful near the edge: unshaded ground outside the covered area has simply not been surveyed, and looks exactly like ground that is under 30 degrees. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The data is stitched together from several countries' national elevation models, and swisstopo, who publish it, note that accuracy is not guaranteed in the places where two of those models meet. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Treat the layer as one input among several, never as a verdict. It says nothing about the snowpack, about what is above you, or about the day's danger rating — and a slope being shaded, or not being shaded, is not permission to ski it. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The data is the “Slope classes over 30°” dataset published by swisstopo, the Swiss Federal Office of Topography. Full attribution is in the colophon. {% endblocktrans %}
