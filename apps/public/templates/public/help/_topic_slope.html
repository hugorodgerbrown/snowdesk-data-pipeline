Since SNOW-1073 the slope layer it documents is the terrain filter's "By slope" mode (SNOW-1109 renamed it from "Colour by slope"), painted from our terrain-class tiles (settings.TERRAIN_CLASS_TILE_URL) rather than swisstopo's pre-rendered raster, so this panel says where the colours come from, why the layer starts at zoom level 12 and why it stops at the Swiss border — see docs/decisions/slope-layer-is-painted-from-our-class-tiles.md. SNOW-1073 also removed the two paragraphs on no-fall passages: SNOW-1095 removed the route detail sheet that listed them, so they described a surface that no longer exists. This panel is where the layer's shortcomings live. The map legend carries the five class swatches and a link here, and nothing else: the caveats are too long to sit in a legend panel and too important to drop, and a reader deciding whether to trust a shading needs the whole answer rather than the half that fits. {% endcomment %} {% load i18n %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The slope layer shades the ground by steepness, so you can see how steep a line is without leaving the map. The bands follow the classification the SLF recommends, the same one you will have seen on Swiss maps: 30–35°, 35–40°, 40–45°, 45–50° and over 50°. Ground under 30° is left unshaded. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Turn it on from the layers menu: tick “Terrain filter”, open its settings with the adjust button beside it and choose “By slope”. The rest of that panel still applies. The slope range says which bands are painted, from 30° up unless you change it, and the aspects and the elevation band narrow it further — so you can colour only the north-facing slopes above 2,400 metres, say, by how steep they are. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The first three bands matter most. The large majority of slab avalanches release on slopes between 30 and 45 degrees, which is why that range is the one to find when a bulletin talks about steep terrain in a particular aspect and elevation. {% endblocktrans %}

{# SNOW-1065: a route's steepness is read on the map line from z14, #} {# coloured by the bands, and one point at a time in the route panel. #} {# The trip page's own map colours its line by the bands too. #}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} A saved route’s steepness is read on the map: zoom in close and the route line itself is coloured in the same classes, one colour for each stretch of the same class; on a trip’s page the line is coloured the same way. Either way the colour is the steepness of the GROUND, never of the track: a skin track zigzagging up a steep face climbs gently along its own length, and colouring it by its own gradient would make a serious slope look easy. Where the line is grey and dashed, the terrain data does not cover it — that is the same coverage limit described below, and it never means the ground is gentle. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The same rule runs the other way, and it surprises people: a flat traverse can be drawn steep. A path cut across a face — a piste traverse, a summer track, a bench — is level underfoot while the ground it crosses is not, and the elevation model describes the hillside rather than the few metres you walk on. So a level stretch of a tour can carry 40 or 50 degree colours. That is the slope above and below you, and on a traverse it is the number that matters. {% endblocktrans %}

{# SNOW-1064/1068: the route panel reads one point: the track's #} {# steepness and how it crosses the slope, then the ground in the #} {# avalanche services' words (EAWS), with flat added. #}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Tap the route, or the top line of its profile, to read one point. The profile marks it with a dot and gives its height and its distance from the start; tap the wheel to clear it. The route panel’s header, just above the profile, gives two things: how steep the track is there and how it crosses the slope — down or up the fall line, or across it, and which side the slope falls — and how steep the ground is, in the words the avalanche services use — moderate under 30 degrees, steep from 30, very steep from 35 and extremely steep from 40. Ground under 5 degrees reads as flat. The two can differ a great deal: a track crossing an extremely steep face can itself be almost level. {% endblocktrans %}

{# SNOW-1073: where the colours come from — the same model and the same #} {# arithmetic behind the route line and the point card, which is the #} {# reason the layer moved off swisstopo's raster. #}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The colours are worked out by Snowdesk from swissALTI3D, the elevation model of Switzerland published by swisstopo, the Swiss Federal Office of Topography. Its heights are resampled to a 5 metre grid, and the steepness of each point is measured across the ground around it. The route line’s colours and the figures in the route panel are worked out from the same heights in the same way, so the layer and a route drawn over it agree about the same hillside. {% endblocktrans %}

{% trans "What it cannot tell you" %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Each point’s steepness is measured across about 30 metres of hillside, so the shading describes the average slope of the ground around a point rather than the ground itself. A short steep roll inside an otherwise gentle face will not appear at any zoom, and neither will a small bench in the middle of a steep one. Zoom in far enough and you will see individual squares of about 7 metres — that is the resolution of the data, not a fault in the map. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} It fades out when you zoom out. The layer is drawn from zoom level 12, roughly a valley across the screen; further out nothing is drawn, and the sheet asks you to zoom in. An empty map at a wide view says nothing about how steep the ground is. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} It stops at the Swiss border. swissALTI3D covers Switzerland, so across the border in France, Italy, Austria or Germany the layer has nothing to show. Near the border, ground the data does not reach is hatched in grey; past the edge of the data altogether the sheet says there is no terrain data here. Either way, unshaded ground there has not been surveyed and is not ground under 30 degrees. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} Treat the layer as one input among several, never as a verdict. It says nothing about the snowpack, about what is above you, or about the day's danger rating — and a slope being shaded, or not being shaded, is not permission to ski it. {% endblocktrans %}

{% blocktrans trimmed %} The elevation data is swisstopo’s swissALTI3D, resampled and derived by Snowdesk. Full attribution is in the colophon. {% endblocktrans %}
